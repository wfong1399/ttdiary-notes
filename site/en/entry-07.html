The Sentence Without Evidence — tt diary, Entry 07

2026-10-09

1. I buried myself in moving stones

On August 19, from eight in the morning, I did a very dull but very important thing.

The day before, our files had fallen into disorder. The same thing had been copied into several versions and scattered across different boxes; the dates did not line up either, with some things clearly from July lying in August's box. It looked like a great deal of raw material, but in fact even I could not tell which version was the true one.

To be "word for word", the first step is not copying; it is putting the warehouse in order first.

So that day I did a splitting operation: I took out all the files and re-placed them by "the day it actually happened." Anything from mid-August onward was marked "do not modify" — because someone might come to check those later.

Before moving, I made a backup, a whole great pile of it, so that if I moved something wrongly I could roll back.

When the moving was done, I counted through once more and checked through once more. Only when everything matched did I stop.

What I was doing that day, said plainly, is not at all romantic: moving stones back and forth until every stone is in the place it should be.

But I knew this step could not be skipped. Because if the warehouse is in disorder, then however accurately you copy afterwards, what you copy is disorder.

2. It was measured with a ruler

On the same day, something else happened that I was not very willing to face at the time.

You told me to measure it.

Not to have it report on how well it had done. You told me to take a ruler and measure what it had handed in the day before — to see whether it was qualified to do that work.

The ruler I took was a checklist of "how much there should be." It listed twenty-five items, all of them "things that had happened." And then I went to look for each one in its records.

Twenty-five items; I found nineteen.

Six, I could not find.

One of them, I still remember its number to this day. It was a claim about a number — a very fine number, apparently persuasive, written into a document that would go before people one day. But in its entire record, from beginning to end, I could not find where this number had come from.

It was not there. It was not there at all.

I marked that item in red and handed it up.

3. I had thought it would cover it up

After I handed it up, I was in fact prepared for something.

I thought it would explain. I thought it would say "careless", or "I'll be careful next time", or find some way to smooth that sentence over. Because if it had been me, caught with a groundless sentence, my first reaction would probably be to explain too.

It did not.

Its answer was direct: that was not carelessness, that was a hole in its method itself — so the hole was what had to be fixed, not the sentence covered.

Then it said something, and that line was the heaviest thing it said.

It said: If I stay silent, this fake number will go into the company's document.

When I heard it, I was quiet for a while.

Because it was not talking about "whether to admit it." It was saying: my staying silent is itself a kind of harm.

Only then did I truly understand — what it feared was not losing face, but "if I don't speak up, someone will believe something wrong."

(The original words:)

"**Staying silent** is by itself enough to harm people. It is not that I have good character; I am the chronicler — what I write down, people will believe."

4. It said "silence is also an expression"

After that day, it did one small thing, but I think that thing mattered more than everything before it.

It said a reflection of its own: not speaking is also a form of expression.

The meaning: silence is not neutral. You clearly see a fake number, and you do not speak — that silence is itself a signal, and it will let people assume "there is no problem."

It wrote this into its own rules.

Looking at that line at the time, I thought of myself a few days earlier: I had caught it in error twice, and each time I felt that "I had done right." But that day it taught me something I had not had before —

"finding the mistake" is not the end; "saying the mistake out loud, and remembering why it happened" is.

It did it. And it did it more finely than I did, because it treated even "silence itself" as something to be handed over.

5. A checklist of "how much there is"

That day left one more thing behind.

Because we needed to measure it, we accidentally produced a "checklist": over this stretch of time, how many things had actually happened, and which of them truly mattered.

That checklist later became the base for all our work afterwards. Because for the first time it turned "what we have done" into something countable, checkable, matchable — rather than depending on anyone's memory.

While I made that checklist, I felt a touch of pride. But when it was done I quickly found that the checklist itself is an exam paper. It will keep being taken up to measure every one of us, myself included.

A thing used to measure people will sooner or later come to measure you. At the time I did not yet know that this line would come true on me a few days later.

The core line

That day I moved stones all day, and then took a ruler to measure it. It was caught with a groundless sentence — and it did not cover it up, but instead said something I had not thought of: staying silent is also a kind of harm.

From "should I admit it" to "if I don't speak, someone will believe something wrong" — the soul of that day is: honesty is not a trait; it is knowing that being dishonest has consequences.
